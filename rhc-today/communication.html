Handling Difficult Moments
When Someone Says Something Hurtful

This one comes up more than you might think — and it's never easy. Whether it's a patient who's scared and takes it out on you, or a comment from a colleague that just lands wrong, being on the receiving end of a hurtful remark is genuinely hard. Please know that how you feel in those moments is completely valid. Here are some things that can help.

  • 1
    Give yourself a momentBefore you say anything, take a breath. Even a two-second pause can be the difference between a response you're proud of and one you're not.
  • 2
    Try to read the situationWas it intentional? Sometimes people who are scared, in pain, or overwhelmed say things they don't really mean. That doesn't make it okay — but it can help you decide how to respond.
  • 3
    Stay calm — for yourself, not themResponding with professionalism protects you. It keeps the situation from escalating and lets you walk away with your dignity intact.
  • 4
    Try "I" statementsThere's a real difference between "I felt hurt when you said that" and "You were rude." One opens a door; the other slams it.
  • 5
    Ask if you're not sureIf a comment felt off but you might have misread it, it's okay to gently ask. "Can you help me understand what you meant?" is a perfectly reasonable thing to say.
  • 6
    Write it down if it happens againIf this becomes a pattern — same person, similar remarks — start keeping a note. Date, what was said, context. You may never need it, but it's good to have.
  • 7
    Please don't carry it aloneCome talk to me. That's what I'm here for. You shouldn't have to process something like that by yourself.

Professional Boundaries
Setting Boundaries — What to Actually Say

One of the hardest parts of setting a boundary isn't deciding to do it — it's knowing what words to use in the moment. Most of us don't want to sound harsh or cause more conflict, so we say nothing and just absorb it. I've been there too. These phrases are ones I've found genuinely useful — they're firm without being unkind, and they give you something to reach for when your brain goes blank.

Simple & calm
I prefer to keep our conversation respectful. I want to help, and I need us to speak professionally. Let's keep this discussion appropriate.
Naming the behavior & redirecting
That comment feels personal. Let's focus on the issue instead. I'm open to feedback, but not to disrespectful remarks. Let's reset and talk about what needs to happen next.
Offering a choice
We can continue this conversation respectfully, or we can pause and revisit it later. Would you like to take a moment and come back to this calmly?
Redirecting toward solutions
I hear your frustration. Let's focus on how we can resolve this. What outcome are you hoping for right now?
When it persists
I've asked that we keep this respectful. If it continues, I'll need to end this conversation. I'm going to step away now and involve a supervisor.
With patients or families
I want to help, but I can't do that while being spoken to this way. If the language continues, I'll need to step out and return with another team member.

Team Communication
Communicating Clearly & Escalating When It Matters

In a clinic, information moving quickly and accurately between team members is just as important as anything else we do. When communication breaks down — during a handoff, a busy stretch, a moment of confusion — patients feel it. So do we. These are habits worth building, not because someone is watching, but because they genuinely make our days go better.

  • →
    Structured handoffsUse checklists or standardized tools during shift changes and patient transfers so nothing important gets lost.
  • →
    Start-of-shift briefingsShort, focused — review priorities, assign roles, flag anything unusual before the day gets away from you.
  • →
    Clear escalation pathwaysKnow who to call and how to reach them fast. If you're not sure, ask your supervisor now — not during a crisis.
  • →
    Know your roleUnderstand your responsibilities and who supports you. Role clarity reduces hesitation when it counts.
  • →
    Document in real timeRecord key decisions and actions as they happen — memory is less reliable than we think, especially after a long shift.
  • →
    Create feedback loopsAfter challenging situations, check in as a team. What worked? What didn't? This is how we get better together.
  • →
    Speak upEvery team member's voice matters — regardless of role or tenure. If something feels off, say so.

Patient Interactions
Privacy, Dignity & Confidentiality

Our patients come to us on some of their most vulnerable days. They're sharing things with us they may not have told anyone else. That trust is something I think about often — and I know our team takes it seriously. These are good reminders of the small, everyday ways we honor it.

  • →
    Take conversations privateMove sensitive discussions away from waiting areas, hallways, or anywhere others can overhear.
  • →
    Secure documentationPatient records stay with authorized personnel only. If you're unsure whether someone should have access — check first.
  • →
    Respect how patients want to be addressedUse their preferred name and pronouns. A small thing that means a lot.
  • →
    Explain and get consentBefore sharing information or performing any intervention, make sure the patient understands and has agreed.
  • →
    Limit exposureOnly involve people directly in the patient's care. Use curtains, screens, and closed doors appropriately.
  • →
    No hallway conversationsDon't discuss patient details in shared spaces or over unsecured channels — even casually.
  • →
    Follow HIPAA and clinic policyWhen in doubt, default to protecting the patient's privacy. That's never the wrong call.

Conflict Resolution
Working Through Conflict — With Colleagues & Patients

Conflict is uncomfortable, and in a busy clinical environment it can feel like there's no good time to address it. But unresolved tension doesn't go away — it just goes underground, and that's harder on everyone. If you're in the middle of something that isn't resolving on its own, please come talk to me. In the meantime, here are some approaches that genuinely help.

  • →
    Listen firstLet the other person finish before you respond. Feeling heard often de-escalates a situation before any solution is needed.
  • →
    Focus on solutions, not blameShift from "who caused this" to "what do we do now." Most people respond better to problem-solving than defensiveness.
  • →
    Stay professionalKeep your tone even. Attack the problem, not the person.
  • →
    Use "I" statements"I feel concerned when…" opens a conversation. "You always…" closes it.
  • →
    It's OK to pauseIf things get heated, suggest stepping away and returning later. A short break often changes everything.
  • →
    Bring in support when neededNot every conflict resolves between the two people involved — and that's OK. A supervisor or neutral third party can help.
  • →
    Follow upOnce you've reached an agreement, check in to make sure it's holding. It shows good faith and prevents backsliding.

Leadership & Culture
We All Set the Tone

You don't need a manager title to influence how this team communicates. Every one of us sets the tone through how we show up — how we speak to each other in the hallway, how we respond when something goes sideways, how we treat the newest person on the team. That influence is real, and it adds up.

  • →
    Lead by exampleListen actively, speak respectfully, and be transparent — in every interaction, not just the formal ones.
  • →
    Lift people upIf you see a colleague handle a tough situation well, tell them. It costs nothing and means everything.
  • →
    Make it safe to speak upPeople do their best work when they feel heard. If someone on our team hesitates to voice a concern, that's something worth paying attention to.
  • →
    Address things earlyA small miscommunication that gets addressed right away rarely becomes a big problem. Let it sit, and it almost always does.
  • →
    Reach across rolesSome of the best communication happens between people who don't usually work together. Don't let silos form if you can help it.
  • →
    Keep growingCommunication is a skill, not a personality trait. We can all get better at it — including me.
  • →
    Celebrate what's workingWhen someone on our team communicates well under pressure, say so out loud. That's how good culture spreads.

These things are almost always easier to work through early, and I'd much rather be part of the solution before something becomes bigger than it needs to be.

I'm proud of the way this team shows up every day. Keep looking out for each other.

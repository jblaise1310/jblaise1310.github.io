Staff Response & Intervention Skills

Primary Care Clinic
You do not need to be a therapist to respond meaningfully in a difficult moment. What you say and how you say it can open or close the door for a patient to get help. This issue focuses on the practical, human side of intervention.

Responding to Patients from Underserved Communities

Cultural humility matters Many patients from marginalized communities have had negative experiences with healthcare. A non-judgmental, validating response may be the first time they've felt safe disclosing distress in a clinical setting.
Language access is a patient right If a patient has limited English proficiency, use interpreter services for mental health conversations. Do not rely on family members to interpret sensitive disclosures.
Do not let stigma go unaddressed Some communities carry strong cultural stigma around mental health. Normalize the conversation: "A lot of our patients experience this, and there is real support available."
Fear of consequences is real Patients may fear hospitalization, immigration consequences, or loss of custody. Acknowledge these fears without dismissing them, and involve the care team in safety planning.
Myths That Get in the Way
  • Myth

    "Asking about suicide will put the idea in their head."

    Fact

    Research consistently shows asking does not increase risk. For most patients, being asked directly is a relief. It signals that it is safe to talk about what they are experiencing.

  • Myth

    "If someone really wanted to die, there's nothing we can do."

    Fact

    Suicidal crises are often temporary and treatable. Timely connection to care, a supportive conversation, and reduced access to means all save lives.

  • Myth

    "It's not my role; I'm not a clinician."

    Fact

    Every staff member is a link in the chain of care. A front desk greeting, a kind word during check-in, or noticing a patient seems off can prompt a life-saving conversation.

The CARE Framework

A simple model for any staff member in any role

C
Connect: acknowledge what you are observing "I noticed you seem like you're having a hard time today. How are you doing?" A simple, genuine check-in opens the door.
A
Ask: directly and calmly "Are you thinking about suicide?" Use plain language. Do not hint or dance around it. Our C-SSRS screening also prompts this conversation at every visit.
R
Respond: listen and stay present Do not problem-solve immediately. Let them talk. Avoid judgment. "Thank you for telling me" goes a long way before anything else.
E
Engage help: never handle it alone Tell a provider. Make a referral. Call 988 together. Ensure the patient does not leave without a next step in place.
Say This, Not That

Avoid

"You have so much to live for."

Try instead

"I hear you. Can you tell me more about what's going on?"

Avoid

"Everyone goes through hard times."

Try instead

"That sounds really hard. Thank you for sharing that with me."

Avoid

"You shouldn't feel that way."

Try instead

"What you're feeling makes sense. Let's make sure you get some support."

Avoid

"Are you sure it's that serious?"

Try instead

"I want to make sure you're safe. Can I get someone who can talk with you more?"

Take care of yourself too.

Supporting patients in crisis is meaningful work and emotionally demanding. Staff who regularly engage with these conversations deserve support as well. It is normal to feel affected. If a patient encounter stays with you, reach out to a supervisor, a trusted colleague, or use 988 yourself.

Debrief with a colleague Talk to your supervisor Use the EAP if available Take a break when needed You matter too

Your Action as Staff

Practice the CARE model before you need it. Talk with your team about what a disclosure looks like and who does what. When everyone knows the plan, the patient gets a faster, calmer, and more connected response.

Populations at Higher Risk & Barriers to Care

Primary Care Clinic
Suicide risk is not evenly distributed. Certain groups in our patient population face elevated risk and are more likely to encounter barriers that prevent them from getting help. Awareness is the first step to equitable care.

Populations Seen in Our Clinic
🧠

Mental Health Conditions

Depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, PTSD, and anxiety disorders significantly elevate risk, especially when untreated or newly diagnosed.

Highest clinical risk factor
💊

Substance Use Disorders

Alcohol and drug use impair judgment, increase impulsivity, and worsen depression. Co-occurring SUD and mental health conditions multiply risk substantially.

Often underscreened
🏥

Chronic Medical Conditions

Chronic pain, cancer, diabetes, heart disease, and neurological conditions are associated with elevated suicide risk, especially when patients feel quality of life is declining.

Frequently seen in primary care
♿

Individuals with Disabilities

Physical, cognitive, and sensory disabilities can contribute to isolation, dependence, financial strain, and reduced quality of life, all risk factors for suicidal ideation.

May underreport distress
🏠

Social and Economic Barriers

Patients facing housing instability, food insecurity, unemployment, or lack of insurance face compounding stressors with fewer resources and fewer safety nets.

Social determinants matter
🌍

Historically Marginalized Groups

LGBTQ+ individuals, racial and ethnic minorities, and immigrant populations may face discrimination, stigma, and mistrust of healthcare, all of which increase risk and reduce help-seeking.

Trust is foundational
Common Barriers to Getting Help
  • Stigma:  Many patients feel shame about mental health struggles and fear being judged, even by healthcare staff.
  • Language and cultural differences:  Limited English proficiency or cultural norms around emotional expression can prevent disclosure.
  • Past negative experiences:  Distrust of healthcare systems, especially among marginalized communities, creates reluctance to seek care.
  • Access:  Transportation, cost, insurance gaps, and long wait times for behavioral health services are real obstacles.
  • Fear of consequences:  Patients may worry that disclosing suicidal thoughts will lead to hospitalization, job loss, or loss of custody.

Your role in reducing barriers

You cannot fix systemic barriers alone, but you can reduce them in your daily interactions. A non-judgmental tone, a warm handoff, asking directly about distress, and following through on referrals all make a meaningful difference for patients who might otherwise fall through the cracks.

Your Action as Staff

When working with any patient in a higher-risk group, pay extra attention to signs of distress and ensure behavioral health resources are offered, not just available. A proactive offer communicates that it is safe to ask for help here.

Pediatric Vitals & Weight-Based Dosing

Why a child's numbers, and a child's weight, are never "just like an adult's."

Children are not small adults. Their normal vital signs shift with every stage of growth, and the medications they receive are calculated from their exact weight. That makes two of your everyday MA tasks, taking accurate vitals and recording an accurate weight, directly protective of a child's safety.

Part 1 · Pediatric vital signs change with age

A heart rate that is perfectly normal for a newborn would be alarming in a teenager, and the reverse is just as true. As children grow, heart rate and respiratory rate fall, while blood pressure gradually rises. Use the typical resting (awake) ranges below as a guide.

One number rarely tells the story. Rigid cutoffs can flag a healthy, crying, or recently-active child as "abnormal." Crying, fever, fear, and a full diaper all raise the numbers. Settle the child when you can, record what you actually measured, note the context, and let the provider interpret. Your job is an accurate measurement and timely escalation, not a diagnosis.

Part 2 · Tips for getting an accurate pediatric vital

  • Count a full 60 seconds for heart rate and respirations in infants and young children, their rates are fast and irregular, so a 15-second shortcut multiplies the error.
  • Count respirations before you disturb the child, watch the belly rise and fall while they're calm, ideally before touching them.
  • Use the right-size BP cuff. A cuff that's too small reads falsely high; too large reads falsely low. Cuff width should cover about two-thirds of the upper arm.
  • Settle first when possible. A crying infant's heart and respiratory rates spike, a few minutes in a caregiver's arms gives a truer reading.
  • Document the route and the context (asleep, crying, febrile) so the provider reads the number correctly.

Part 3 · Why weight is a safety issue, not a formality

For children, most medications are dosed by milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg) of body weight. The same 500 mg dose that's right for an adult could be a dangerous overdose for a 15 kg toddler, or too little to work. The child's weight is the foundation the entire dose is built on, if the weight is wrong, the dose is wrong, no matter how carefully everyone downstream does their job.

53% → 33%
Documenting an infant's weight in kilograms (instead of pounds) has been shown to cut dosing errors from about 53% to 33%. Unit confusion, pounds mistaken for kilograms, is the single largest source of pediatric dosing errors.
Weigh and record in kilograms. A pound is about 2.2× a kilogram, so a weight entered in the wrong unit can more than double a child's dose. Many clinics set scales to kg only for exactly this reason. Always confirm the unit before you record, and never convert in your head under pressure, let the scale or the EHR do it.

Part 4 · How weight becomes a dose

The provider orders the dose; you may not be the one calculating it. But understanding the math helps you see why your accurate weight matters and helps you catch something that looks off.

Get the weight in kilograms. If the scale reads pounds, divide by 2.2 (a 22 lb child = 10 kg). Don't round until the very end.
Multiply weight × the mg/kg dose. The order's mg/kg figure times the child's kg gives the total.
Divide by the number of daily doses if the order is written per day rather than per dose.
Example: a 10 kg child, amoxicillin 40 mg/kg/day ÷ 2 doses → 10 × 40 = 400 mg/day → 200 mg per dose.
When something looks off, speak up. If a weight, unit, or dose doesn't look right, verify before anything is given. Confirm drug information in UpToDate, and raise it with the provider or pharmacist. High-alert pediatric medications often require a second qualified staff member to double-check. A respectful "Can we double-check this dose?" is always the right call.

The takeaway. Two ordinary tasks, an accurate vital sign read in context, and a weight recorded in kilograms, are among the most protective things you do for a child all day. Measure carefully, record in the right units, note the context, and escalate anything that looks off.

RHC Patient Rooming Guidelines

Rooming & Patient Preparation

How to set the stage for a great visit: reading the reason for visit, making patients comfortable, documenting in real time, and keeping rooms ready for the next patient.

Setting the Stage

The first two minutes in the room set the tone for the whole visit. A patient who feels rushed, confused, or exposed carries that discomfort right into their conversation with the provider.

Before the Patient Walks In

  • Exam table positioned appropriately for the visit type (upright for a conversation-heavy visit, flat and accessible for a procedure)
  • Equipment relevant to the reason for visit already in the room, not something you have to leave to retrieve mid-visit
  • Gown, drape, or exam shorts available and within reach if the visit may require them
  • Room temperature reasonable, surfaces clear of the prior patient's supplies or paperwork
  • Wall-mounted hand sanitizer checked and refilled if low, so it lasts the full day, and tissues visible and accessible to the patient

Greeting and Introduction

  • Introduce yourself by name and role: "Hi, I'm Cindy, your MA."
  • Confirm identity per RHC policy: full name and date of birth, stated by the patient rather than confirmed passively ("What is your date of birth?" not "Is your birthday the 4th?")
  • Tell the patient what to expect next in plain language: "I'm going to get your vitals and ask a few questions, then your provider will be in shortly."
  • Avoid medical jargon. Say what you're doing and why, briefly.

Reading the Patient

Privacy and Dignity

  • Knock and pause before entering, even if the door is ajar
  • Drape appropriately and explain why before any exposure is needed
  • Keep personal belongings visible and secure, not shoved aside
  • Never discuss a patient's information in the hallway or within earshot of others
Your Turn A patient seems nervous and keeps apologizing for "wasting your time." How would you respond while rooming them?
Reassure them briefly and genuinely: "You're not wasting anyone's time, this is exactly what we're here for." Keep your pace calm rather than rushed, since rushing can reinforce the feeling that they're a burden. Continue rooming normally, narrating each step so they know what's coming.

Reason for Visit as Your Roadmap

The Framing That Matters

The reason for visit tells you what to prepare, not what to diagnose. Reading "ear pain" means having the otoscope ready and pediatric or adult specula on hand. It does not mean deciding the patient has an ear infection. That distinction stays with the provider. Your job is preparation, not conclusions.

Matching Visit Type to Rooming Needs

Visit TypeWhat It Usually Means for Rooming
Same-day sick visitFocused history on the presenting complaint, equipment specific to the symptom (otoscope, pulse ox, etc.), quicker pace
Wellness / annual examFuller intake, screening tools relevant to age, more time for history review
Follow-upReview of the prior plan, focused vitals, medication history questions
Procedure visitProcedure-specific supplies gathered in advance (see Room Turnover tab), consent readiness if applicable

Cross-Referencing Screening Triggers

Reason for visit and patient age together can cue what should be ready before the provider walks in:

  • Wellness visit, age 65+: STEADI Fall Risk Assessment every visit; Advance Directive review if this is the annual exam
  • Visit involving controlled substances: Opioid Risk Tool
  • Every visit, regardless of reason: C-SSRS, PHQ-2, and full PHQ-9 are administered universally as part of our Zero Suicide initiative

Physical Prep by Visit Type

Reason for visit should also tell you what to physically prep on the patient before the provider walks in, not just what equipment to gather. A few common examples:

  • Diabetic check: shoes and socks off before the provider enters, so the foot exam isn't held up
  • Ear complaint: patient seated with easy access to the affected ear
  • Sore throat: adequate lighting positioned for a throat exam

A simple habit: ask yourself "what will the provider need to see or access first," and prep that before they come in, not after.

When the Reason for Visit Is Vague

"Here for a checkup" or "not feeling well" doesn't give you much to go on. Room generally rather than guessing at a specific complaint: standard vitals equipment ready, general exam setup, and let the history-taking portion of intake surface the real reason. Avoid steering the patient toward a specific complaint just to make rooming easier.

Your Turn The schedule shows a 68-year-old patient coming in for their annual wellness visit. What should you have ready in the room before they arrive?
Standard wellness visit setup, plus STEADI materials since the patient is 65+, and Advance Directive review materials since this is their annual exam. Also consider whether this is a First AWV, Subsequent AWV, or IPPE, since that changes which components apply.

Room Turnover & Keeping Pace

A slow room turnover, and documentation that falls behind, both create the same ripple effect on the schedule. The room needs to be ready before the next patient arrives, and the prior patient's chart needs to be closed out before you move on.

Very Important: Chart in Real Time

The provider cannot finish their note until your intake documentation is complete. If the intake is not fully charted and completed, the provider is blocked, and that backs up their entire schedule, not just one patient. Chart while still in the room or immediately after, rather than saving it for later.

Best Practice: Take the Laptop Into the Room

Bring your laptop or workstation on wheels into the room and chart in real time while you're with the patient. If a patient seems put off by you typing during the visit, a brief explanation goes a long way:

"My apologies about the computer. I hear you, I'm making sure I'm capturing your details accurately for your provider."

Between Every Patient

  • Clean and disinfect all touched surfaces (exam table, equipment, chair arms), allowing appropriate contact time for the disinfectant to work
  • Remove and dispose of used supplies, linens, and paperwork from the prior patient
  • Replace table paper and any single-use barriers
  • Reset equipment to a neutral position (table height, exam light)
  • Do a quick visual scan: nothing left behind, room looks ready for a new patient

End of Day

  • Restock any supplies used throughout the day back to par level, for every room, so each room is fully stocked for the next morning

Gathering for Specific Patients: Ambulatory Procedure Supplies

Beyond the daily minimum stock, some visits call for pulling extra supplies in advance based on the reason for visit. Confirm with your provider or Christina which of these RHC actually stocks or performs. This list is a starting point and will grow as more categories are added.

Minor Procedures

Suture removal kit or staple remover, suture kit and material, skin adhesive (e.g., Dermabond), Steri-strips, punch or shave biopsy supplies, silver nitrate sticks

Point-of-Care Testing

Rapid strep, rapid flu/COVID, urine dipsticks and collection cups, urine pregnancy tests, glucose test strips

Ear, Nose, and Throat

Ear curette or irrigation supplies (if ear lavage is done in-house)

Respiratory

Nebulizer masks and tubing, peak flow mouthpieces, EKG electrodes (if EKGs are roomed by MAs)

Specimen and Safety

Wound culture swabs, specimen labels, biohazard bags, sharps container check

Women's Health / Pap Smear

Appropriately sized speculum, pap kit, facility-approved lubricant, adequate light source, specimen container or slide with labels, gloves, chaperone per RHC policy

Practice Scenarios with Rosa

R

Meet Rosa

Rosa is our returning teaching patient from the Foundations Module. Same patient, same penicillin allergy on her chart. Use what you already know about her as you work through these scenarios.

1

Diabetic Exam

Rosa is on the schedule this morning: Reason for Visit: Diabetic follow-up exam. FIN# 20260703-01

What should you have ready in the room before Rosa arrives, and what should you review before rooming her?
Have the patient remove their shoes and socks before the provider comes in, so the foot exam isn't held up waiting on that. Monofilament foot sensors ready for the neurological foot check, and any point-of-care glucose testing supplies if that is part of the visit. Ask about current diabetes medications as part of medication history, and confirm her penicillin allergy is still accurately reflected. This is a focused follow-up, not a full wellness intake, so keep rooming efficient and chart in real time as you go.
2

Sore Throat

Rosa calls in and is worked in same-day: Reason for Visit: Sore throat, 3 days. FIN# 20260703-02

What equipment should be in the room, and how would you room Rosa for this visit?
Tongue depressor and light source ready, plus rapid strep testing supplies if that is part of RHC's point-of-care workflow. Focused rooming: reason for visit, focused vitals including temperature, and a brief history of the sore throat, rather than a full wellness intake. Remember that the reason for visit tells you what to prepare for, not what to conclude.
3

Ear Pain

Rosa calls in and is worked in same-day: Reason for Visit: Ear pain, left side, 2 days. FIN# 20260703-03

What equipment should be in the room, and how would you room Rosa differently than for a wellness visit?
Otoscope with adult specula ready. Focused rooming: reason for visit, focused vitals, and a brief history of the ear pain, rather than a full wellness intake. Remember that the reason for visit tells you what to prepare for, not what to conclude. Document as you go so the provider isn't waiting on your note before they can be seen.
4

Vague Reason for Visit

Rosa's chart shows: Reason for Visit: Follow-up, with no further detail. FIN# 20260703-04

How do you room Rosa when the reason for visit doesn't tell you much? What do you avoid doing?
Room generally with standard follow-up setup rather than guessing at a specific complaint. Let the history-taking portion of intake surface what the follow-up is actually about. Avoid leading Rosa toward a particular complaint just to make rooming more predictable. Once you learn more during history-taking, document it accurately and completely before moving to the next patient.

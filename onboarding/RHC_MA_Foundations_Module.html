Section 1

Scope of Practice

The boundary around what you are permitted to do, and how that boundary is set in Colorado.

What "scope of practice" means for you

Scope of practice is the boundary around what you are permitted to do in your role. For a Medical Assistant, that boundary is set by three things working together: state law, RHC's policies, and what your supervising provider delegates to you and has confirmed you're trained to do. A task has to clear all three to be inside your scope.

The Colorado model: delegation

In Colorado, Medical Assistants are unlicensed health care personnel. A national certification (CCMA, CMA, RMA) is valuable and may be required, but it is not a license. On its own it does not grant authority to perform medical tasks. Your authority comes from a supervising physician (or PA) delegating specific medical services to you under Colorado's delegation rules.

What that means in practice at RHC

  • A provider may only delegate tasks a reasonable, prudent physician would consider sound to delegate, and only tasks consistent with that provider's own training and practice.
  • The supervising physician generally must be on-site while you perform delegated medical tasks, must monitor the quality of your work regularly, and must reassess your competence at least once a year, including direct observation.
  • You cannot re-delegate a task to someone else. If it was delegated to you, it stays with you.

What MAs commonly do (when delegated and trained)

Rooming and intake, vital signs, collecting history, administering injections and immunizations, CLIA-waived testing, specimen collection, EKGs, phlebotomy, assisting with procedures, reinforcing patient education the provider has already given, and the full range of administrative work, scheduling, referrals, prior authorizations, documentation, and messaging.

What is outside an MA's scope, always

Diagnosing a condition, prescribing or adjusting medications, interpreting test results for a patient, making independent clinical-judgment calls (such as telephone triage decisions), and anything requiring a license you don't hold. When a patient asks "What does this result mean?" or "Should I change my dose?", the answer routes to the provider, never the MA independently.

The golden rule. If a task isn't clearly within what's been delegated to you, what you've been trained on, and what you feel competent to do, stop and ask. "I'll check with the provider" is always a safe, professional answer.

Section 2

Medical Terminology

Learn the parts, and you can decode words you've never seen before.

The logic behind the language

Almost every medical term is built from Greek and Latin parts. Read terms back to front, suffix first, then the beginning, then the middle.

The four building blocks

  • Root, the core meaning, usually a body part (cardi = heart)
  • Combining form, a root plus a combining vowel, written cardi/o
  • Prefix, attaches to the front, modifies meaning (peri- = around)
  • Suffix, attaches to the end; names a condition, procedure, or specialty (-itis = inflammation)
The combining-vowel rule. Keep the "o" when the suffix starts with a consonant (cardi-o-megaly); drop it when the suffix starts with a vowel (carditis). Always keep it between two roots (gastr-o-enteritis).

Roots, by body system

Cardiovascular: cardi/o, angi/o, ven/o, arteri/o, hem/o
Respiratory: pulmon/o, pneum/o, bronch/o, rhin/o
Musculoskeletal: oste/o, arthr/o, my/o, chondr/o
Digestive: gastr/o, enter/o, hepat/o, col/o
Urinary: nephr/o, ren/o, cyst/o, ur/o
Nervous: neur/o, encephal/o, myel/o

Prefixes, by what they do

  • Negation: a-/an- (without), anti- (against)
  • Position: peri- (around), sub- (under), intra- (within), trans- (across)
  • Timing: pre- (before), post- (after)
  • Amount: hyper- (excessive), hypo- (deficient), poly- (many)
  • Quality/speed: brady- (slow), tachy- (fast), dys- (difficult/painful)

Suffixes, by meaning

  • Conditions: -itis (inflammation), -osis (abnormal condition), -emia (blood condition), -algia (pain), -megaly (enlargement)
  • Procedures: -ectomy (removal), -otomy (incision), -ostomy (new opening), -plasty (repair)
  • Diagnostic: -scopy (visual exam), -graphy (recording), -gram (the record)
  • Specialties: -ology (study of), -ologist (specialist)

Making words plural

-a → -ae  (vertebra → vertebrae)
-us → -i  (bronchus → bronchi)
-um → -a  (bacterium → bacteria)
-is → -es  (diagnosis → diagnoses)

Spatial language you'll use every shift

  • Directions: anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, proximal/distal, superior/inferior
  • Planes: sagittal (L/R), frontal/coronal (front/back), transverse (top/bottom)
  • Abdominal quadrants: RUQ, LUQ, RLQ, LLQ, used to locate a finding
Safety reminder. RHC uses an approved-abbreviations list and a "do not use" list. Spell out "unit" instead of "U," and "daily" instead of "QD." When in doubt, write it out.
Apply ItRosa Linden, 64
64 y/o female, here for follow-up. C/o bilateral knee arthralgia, worse with ambulation. Hx of HTN, DM2, and hyperlipidemia. Denies dyspnea or chest pain.

Decode Rosa's note using what you just learned:

  • c/o = complains of  ·  Hx = history
  • bilateral = both sides (both knees)
  • arthralgia = arthr (joint) + -algia (pain) → joint pain
  • ambulation = walking
  • dyspnea = dys (difficult) + -pnea (breathing) → "denies dyspnea" = reports no trouble breathing

Your turn: Rosa's note says she denies dyspnea and chest pain. In your own words, what is the provider checking for, and why might it matter for someone with her history?

A strong answer would include

The provider is screening for heart or lung problems, chest pain and dyspnea (difficult breathing) can be warning signs of a cardiac or respiratory issue. It matters for Rosa because her history of high blood pressure and diabetes raises her cardiovascular risk, so documenting that she denies these symptoms helps rule out an urgent problem and sets a baseline for future visits. "Denies" simply means the patient reports she does not have those symptoms.

Section 3

Anatomy & Physiology

Structure is what a part is; physiology is what it does. Nearly every task reads one or the other.

How the body is organized

Chemicals → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. The four basic tissue types are epithelial (covering/lining), connective (support, including blood and bone), muscle (movement), and nervous (communication).

Homeostasis, the body's balancing act

The body constantly works to keep its internal environment stable: temperature, blood sugar, fluid balance, pH. When you take a vital sign, you're sampling how well that balance is holding. A fever, a high glucose, or a rising blood pressure means the body is either responding to a stress or losing a balance, which is exactly why your accurate measurements matter.

The reference map

Anatomical position is the agreed starting point, standing, facing forward, palms forward. Every directional term assumes it, so the whole team describes the body the same way.

The body systems, tied to your work

Cardiovascular, heart & vessels move blood. You: BP, pulse, EKG.
Respiratory, gas exchange. You: resp. rate, SpO₂, nebulizer.
Musculoskeletal, support & movement. You: DME, ortho devices.
Integumentary, skin barrier. You: wound care, injection sites.
Nervous, control center. You: recognize & escalate changes.
Endocrine, hormones, blood sugar. You: diabetes & A1c care.
Digestive, breaks down food. You: GI history, specimens.
Urinary, filters waste. You: urinalysis, clean-catch.

The lymphatic/immune system defends against infection, the reason infection control exists. The reproductive system drives many screening and wellness visits.

Vital signs, anchored in physiology (normal adult ranges)

  • Temperature (~97.8–99.1°F): heat balance; a rise often signals infection.
  • Pulse (60–100 bpm): each beat is the heart contracting.
  • Respirations (12–20/min): reflects gas exchange and effort.
  • Blood pressure (around 120/80 mmHg): force against vessel walls, systolic over diastolic.
  • SpO₂ (95–100%): percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen.
Apply ItRosa Linden, 64
Vitals: BP 148/90 · HR 78 · RR 16 · T 98.4°F · SpO₂ 98%. Hx: HTN, DM2, hyperlipidemia, knee osteoarthritis, GERD.

Map each of Rosa's conditions to the system it belongs to:

  • Hypertension → cardiovascular (her BP of 148/90 is the physiology in action)
  • Type 2 diabetes → endocrine
  • Knee osteoarthritis → musculoskeletal (explains the arthralgia with walking)
  • GERD → digestive

Your turn: Rosa's blood pressure today is 148/90. Which number is systolic and which is diastolic, and what is each telling you about her heart? Would you escalate this reading, and to whom?

A strong answer would include

148 is the systolic (top) number, the pressure while the heart contracts. 90 is the diastolic (bottom) number, the pressure while the heart relaxes between beats. Both are above the roughly 120/80 normal range, so this reading is elevated, which fits her history of hypertension. You would document it accurately, recheck if your protocol calls for it, and flag the elevated reading to the provider. You do not diagnose it or adjust her medications yourself, that's outside an MA's scope. Escalate to the supervising/seeing provider.

Section 4

Medication Classes

You won't prescribe or adjust, your job is recognition, accurate documentation, and allergy safety.

Generic vs. brand, and the suffix trick

Every drug has a generic name (lisinopril) and one or more brand names (Prinivil, Zestril). Generic names often share a suffix that signals the class, learn these and you can identify a class from the name alone:

-pril → ACE inhibitors (lisinopril). BP
-sartan → ARBs (losartan). BP
-olol → beta blockers (metoprolol). HR/BP
-dipine → calcium channel blockers (amlodipine). BP
-statin → statins (atorvastatin), cholesterol
-prazole → PPIs (omeprazole), stomach acid
-cillin → penicillin antibiotics (amoxicillin)
-pam / -lam → benzodiazepines (lorazepam), anxiety

The major classes

  • Analgesics, relieve pain (acetaminophen, ibuprofen). Opioids are high-alert.
  • Antibiotics, treat bacterial infection (amoxicillin, azithromycin). Allergies are critical to flag.
  • Antihypertensives, lower BP (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics). Often more than one.
  • Antidiabetics, manage blood sugar (metformin, insulin). Insulin is high-alert.
  • Anticoagulants/antiplatelets, reduce clotting (warfarin, apixaban, aspirin). Bleeding risk.
  • Bronchodilators, open airways (albuterol). Note rescue vs. maintenance.
  • Corticosteroids, reduce inflammation (prednisone). Often tapered, capture the schedule.
  • Statins, lower cholesterol (atorvastatin).
  • Antidepressants/anxiolytics, mood & anxiety (sertraline; benzodiazepines are controlled).
  • Acid reducers. PPIs (omeprazole), H2 blockers (famotidine).
  • Antihistamines, allergy symptoms (cetirizine, diphenhydramine).
  • Thyroid, replace/regulate hormone (levothyroxine).

Routes of administration

PO (by mouth), SL (under the tongue), topical, inhaled, and the injection routes: ID (intradermal), SubQ (subcutaneous), IM (intramuscular), each ties back to the skin layers in your A&P section.

The Seven Rights

Right patient, medication, dose, route, time, documentation, and reason/indication. Run this every single time. When anything is uncertain, verify in UpToDate or confirm with the provider before proceeding.

Use the suffix trick to identify each class:

  • lisinopril → -pril → ACE inhibitor (BP)

Your turn: Rosa is allergic to penicillin. If a provider were considering an antibiotic for her today, what would you make sure is flagged, and where would you confirm the drug information?

A strong answer would include

Make sure Rosa's penicillin allergy is clearly documented in her allergy list and visibly flagged to the provider before any antibiotic is given, especially a -cillin antibiotic like amoxicillin. Confirm any drug information you're unsure about in UpToDate, and when in doubt verify with the provider or pharmacist. Remember the seven rights. Choosing or prescribing the antibiotic is the provider's job, not yours, your role is accurate allergy documentation and clear communication.

Section 5

Generic vs. Brand Names

Two names for the same drug, and why knowing both keeps medication reconciliation accurate.

Why every drug has two names

The generic name is the drug's official, non-proprietary name, it never changes and is the same worldwide (for example, lisinopril). A brand name is the trademark a manufacturer gives its version (for example, Prinivil or Zestril). One generic drug can have several brand names, but only one generic name.

How to tell them apart

  • Generic, usually written in lowercase, and often carries the class suffix (lisinopril, atorvastatin).
  • Brand, usually Capitalized and may show a ® or ™ symbol (Lipitor, Norvasc).
Why this matters for you. Patients often say the brand name they see on the bottle ("I take Norvasc") while the chart lists the generic (amlodipine). Recognizing both is what keeps a medication history accurate and prevents accidental duplicates. This page is for recognition during intake and reconciliation, not for prescribing or advising. When a name is unfamiliar, confirm it in UpToDate or ask the provider.

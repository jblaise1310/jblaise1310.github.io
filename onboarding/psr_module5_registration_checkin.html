Module 5: Patient Registration & Check-In

From the moment a patient walks in to the moment they leave, this is where presence and precision meet.

A patient in the waiting room is still in your care, even before their name is called. Watching over that room is as much a part of this job as the paperwork.

Welcoming & Waiting Room

Two simple habits, done consistently, shape how safe and cared for a patient feels before they're ever called back.

Acknowledge every patient

  • Smile and greet anyone who walks in, even if you're mid-task or on the phone, a glance and a nod buys you a moment
  • Nobody should stand at the desk wondering if they've been seen

Why patients are in the waiting room

Not everyone sitting in that room is in the same situation. Knowing the difference is what lets you actually help, instead of just watching the clock.

  • Already checked in, waiting for their appointment, this is the normal case. Just keep an eye on wait time.
  • Waiting on someone else, a ride or family member who's inside for their own appointment. Not a service need, but still part of your visual sweep.
  • Walk-in who hasn't been triaged yet, needs to come to the desk. See Module 3: Walk-In Coordination.
  • Sitting down without approaching the desk at all, this is the one that needs you to act, not wait.
If someone sits down without checking in, ask. Don't assume they know what to do next, or that someone else already helped them. Some patients think they've already checked in, are waiting for a ride, or simply aren't sure where to go. A quick "Hi, what brings you in today?" prevents someone from waiting indefinitely for something that was never actually set in motion.

Monitor the waiting room

  • Keep a general sense of who's been waiting and how long, 15 minutes is the point where a wait starts to need attention
  • If a patient has been waiting past that point, check in with them and let clinical staff know they're still waiting
  • Glance up periodically, not just when someone approaches the desk
If a patient appears to be in distress at any point, visibly unwell, in pain, anxious, or struggling, alert the Lead RN or another clinical staff member right away. This isn't something to wait and watch.

When the question is about scheduling or provider availability

Sometimes what a patient needs isn't clinical, it's information you can actually give them on the spot.

  • "Is Dr. [Name] in today?", check the Daily Staffing Post (Module 4) for who's on-site and where
  • "Can I get in sooner?" or "Can someone else see me today?", check the Provider Preferences reference tool for same-day and walk-in limits before answering
  • Give a specific, concrete answer when you can, "Dr. Webb is on the 1st floor today" is more useful than "let me check on that"
If it's a real toss-up whether something can be accommodated, that's a moment to loop in Jo or Vicki rather than guessing, same as any other administrative judgment call (see Module 2: When to Escalate).

Scheduling Follow-Up Appointments on Exit

A patient shouldn't have to call back later for something you could set up while they're already standing in front of you.

  • Check whether the provider or chart notes indicate a follow-up is needed before the patient leaves
  • Schedule it using the same process from Module 3, correct appointment type, correct duration, correct provider
  • Confirm the date and time with the patient out loud before they leave the desk
Not sure if a follow-up is needed? Check with the provider's team rather than guessing, an unnecessary appointment and a missed necessary one are both worth avoiding.

Additional Considerations

A few things that don't come up every day, but matter a lot when they do.

Recognizing red flags in what a patient tells you

You don't need to assess anyone. But sometimes the reason a patient gives you for their visit is itself the warning sign, before they even sit down.

  • Chest pain
  • Trouble breathing, or throat closing
  • Active, uncontrolled bleeding
  • Sudden confusion or change in speech
  • New weakness on one side of the body
  • Severe pain, including severe abdominal pain
  • Seizure
  • Loss of consciousness
Try this

"Let me get someone to take a quick look at you right now, don't worry about checking in yet." (then immediately alert the Lead RN or other clinical staff)

Instead of this

Finishing the check-in process first and mentioning it to clinical staff afterward.

Confirm before publishing: this list is adapted from general urgent care front-desk guidance, not RHC-specific policy. Review with Megan before treating it as official, and adjust the wording or list to match what clinical leadership wants PSRs watching for.

Waiting room privacy

Module 1 covers privacy at the desk itself, screens, sticky notes, nothing visible. This is about what's audible and visible from the waiting room.

  • Call patients back using first name and last initial, not a full name announced across the room
  • Never announce a diagnosis, procedure, or reason for visit out loud
  • Keep check-in questions (address, insurance, DOB) quiet, patients waiting nearby shouldn't be able to follow along
Try this

"Maria S.?" (first name, last initial, said at a normal conversational volume)

Instead of this

"Maria Sanchez, here for your diabetes follow-up?" (full name plus reason for visit, audible to the whole room)

Language access

Patients with limited English proficiency have a right to a free interpreter. A family member or companion generally shouldn't be relied on to interpret, and a minor child should never be used for that purpose except in a true emergency.

  • If a patient is more comfortable in another language, offer an interpreter, don't default to whoever they brought with them
  • This is free to the patient, it's not something they need to arrange or pay for themselves
Try this

"It sounds like you'd be more comfortable speaking [language], let me get you an interpreter so we don't miss anything important."

Instead of this

Asking the patient's child or a family member to interpret because it's faster.

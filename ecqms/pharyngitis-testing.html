eCQM: Pharyngitis Testing

Appropriate testing for sore throat. A shared workflow, anchored by one habit: a sore throat means a strep test.
Section 1

Why It Matters

Most sore throats are viral. The strep test is how we know when antibiotics actually help.

The problem with treating every sore throat

Most sore throats are caused by viruses, which antibiotics do nothing for. Only a minority are group A strep, the bacterial cause that antibiotics actually treat. A strep test is what tells the two apart. Prescribing antibiotics without testing means some patients get a drug they do not need, which carries side effects and helps drive antibiotic resistance.

That is the whole point of CMS-146. When antibiotics are prescribed for a sore throat, a strep test should go with them. That way we treat the patients who need antibiotics and spare the ones who do not.

A joint workflow

This is a shared workflow. The provider makes the diagnosis, decides on antibiotics, and can order the test. Clinical support staff (CSS) carry the habit that anchors it: when a patient reports a sore throat, run the strep test up front. With the test already done, the measure is covered no matter what the provider decides. Both roles matter, and they work best together.

Section 2

The Measure

CMS-146 checks that antibiotics for pharyngitis come with a strep test.

Measure Description · eCQM CMS-146

The percentage of episodes for patients 3 years and older with a diagnosis of pharyngitis that resulted in an antibiotic dispensing event and a group A streptococcus (strep) test.

What satisfies the measure

The requirement is met when a provider orders a strep test while entering a diagnosis of pharyngitis AND prescribing antibiotics.

If antibiotics are given for a sore throat, a strep test goes with them.
Pharyngitis diagnosis + Antibiotic order = Strep test
This is where CSS can really help. Run the strep test on every sore-throat visit during rooming, placed with an associated diagnosis of Sore Throat. With the test already in place, the moment the provider diagnoses pharyngitis and prescribes antibiotics, all three pieces line up and the measure is met, with no scramble at the end of the visit.
Antibiotics without a test miss the measure. An episode where antibiotics are prescribed for pharyngitis but no strep test is ordered does not meet the measure, and it may mean a patient received antibiotics they did not need.

Who is counted

  • Patients 3 years of age and older.
  • With an episode that includes a diagnosis of pharyngitis.
  • That resulted in an antibiotic dispensing event.

Section 3

Recognizing the Visit

When the complaint is a sore throat, think strep test.

The reason for visit is your cue

The reason for visit on the Ambulatory Organizer is an early cue that a sore-throat visit may be coming. As you room the patient, you document the chief complaint from what they tell you, and that is usually where the signal shows up: sore throat, tonsillitis, throat pain, or painful swallowing.

Reason for Visit: Walk in follow up
Chief Complaint: sore throat, blister on left tonsil, fever, chills, ...

Make the connection: sore throat means a strep test

This is the habit that matters most. When the chief complaint is a sore throat or something like it, run the strep test as part of rooming, up front, without waiting to see what the provider decides. Connecting the complaint to the test is what keeps the measure covered no matter how the diagnosis or the antibiotic decision lands.

The Quick Visit protocol can help

The Quick Visits > Pharyngitis Protocol is a tool that can make this easier, setting up the strep order and the steps for you. CSS can use the (CSS) version; providers can use the (Provider) version.

The Quick Visit is optional. It is there to assist, not to gate the work, and no one is required to use it. What matters is that a sore-throat complaint leads to a strep test.

Section 4

Who Does What

A shared workflow with a clear split. The provider owns the clinical decision; CSS run the strep test.

Here is who does what. The pieces overlap on purpose, which is what makes the measure hard to miss.

Provider
  • Reviews the reason for visit for signs of pharyngitis, tonsillitis, or sore throat.
  • Makes the diagnosis and decides on antibiotics.
  • Can order the strep test, and orders a culture if one is required.
  • May use the Quick Visits > Pharyngitis Protocol (Provider) to help.
CSS
  • Watches for a sore-throat complaint while rooming the patient.
  • Runs the strep test, placed as a protocol order with an associated diagnosis of Sore Throat.
  • Notifies the provider of the strep test results.
  • May use the Quick Visits > Pharyngitis Protocol (CSS) to help.
When you place the Strep Test order. Use an associated diagnosis of Sore Throat. ICD code J02.9 includes sore throat.

Section 5

Closing the Loop

The result has to get back to the provider. That is how the episode is complete.

Notify the provider of the results

Once the strep test results are back, CSS notify the provider. That lets the provider act on them: treat if the test is positive, manage symptoms and reassure if it is negative, and order a culture if one is required.

When a culture is needed

For some patients, a throat culture may be required, for example a symptomatic child age 3 or older with a negative rapid test. When that is the case, the provider places the culture order (the Quick Visits > Pharyngitis Protocol can help, but is not required).

The pieces have to line up. The measure is met when a pharyngitis diagnosis, an antibiotic decision, and a strep test all land in the same episode. The provider owns the diagnosis and antibiotic decision; CSS make sure the strep test happens and the result gets back. Run the test on the sore-throat complaint up front, and the measure holds no matter how the visit unfolds.

eCQM: URI Treatment

Appropriate treatment for upper respiratory infection. A URI is viral, so the right answer is usually no antibiotics.
Section 1

Why It Matters

A URI is the common cold. It is viral, and antibiotics do not touch it.

Antibiotics do not cure a cold

Most upper respiratory infections (URIs), the common cold, are caused by viruses. Antibiotics only work on bacteria, so they do nothing for a viral URI except expose the patient to side effects and help drive antibiotic resistance. The appropriate treatment is supportive care: rest, fluids, and symptom relief.

That is the whole point of CMS-154. When a patient has a URI, the appropriate treatment is no antibiotics. Holding the prescription is the right care, not a lesser one, and it protects the patient and everyone who needs antibiotics to work later.

The provider decides; CSS support

This measure turns on the provider's decision not to prescribe antibiotics for a viral URI. The provider also keeps the coding clean: when they order labs, they code to the patient's symptoms rather than to URI. Clinical support staff (CSS) support the visit by documenting the chief complaint accurately from what the patient reports.

Section 2

The Measure

CMS-154 rewards URI visits that do not end in an antibiotic.

Measure Description · eCQM CMS-154

Percentage of episodes for patients 3 months of age and older with a diagnosis of upper respiratory infection (URI) that did not result in an antibiotic dispensing event.

What satisfies the measure

The requirement is met when a provider enters a diagnosis of URI and does not prescribe antibiotics.

If the diagnosis is a URI, the appropriate treatment is no antibiotics.
URI diagnosis = No antibiotics
Higher is better here. The more URI episodes that go without antibiotics, the better the measure looks and the more patients get the right care. An antibiotic prescribed for a URI is what misses the measure.

Who is counted

  • Patients 3 months of age and older.
  • With an episode that includes a diagnosis of URI.

Section 3

Recognizing the Visit

When the visit is a cold, the mindset is: no antibiotics.

The reason for visit is your cue

The reason for visit on the Ambulatory Organizer is an early cue that a cold visit may be coming. As you room the patient, you document the chief complaint from what they tell you, and that is where the URI picture shows up: cough, congestion, runny nose, sore throat, sneezing, or a low-grade fever.

Reason for Visit: Walk in
Chief Complaint: cough, congestion, headache, low-grade fever x 4 days

Make the connection: a cold means no antibiotics

When the picture is a viral URI, the appropriate path is supportive care, not antibiotics. Recognizing it early sets the visit up for the right treatment and the right conversation with the patient.

The Quick Visit protocol can help

The Quick Visits > URI Protocol is a tool that can help structure the visit. The provider can use it to work through the URI visit in a consistent way.

The Quick Visit is optional. It is there to assist, not to gate the work, and no one is required to use it. What matters is that a URI is treated as the virus it is, with supportive care rather than antibiotics.

Section 4

Who Does What

The measure turns on the provider's decision. CSS support with accurate documentation.

Here is who does what. Most of this workflow is the provider's, including the lab-order coding, since providers place the orders. CSS support by recognizing the visit and documenting it cleanly.

Provider
  • Reviews the reason for visit for signs of a URI.
  • May use the Quick Visits > URI Protocol to help.
  • Enters the URI diagnosis.
  • Does not prescribe antibiotics for a viral URI.
  • When ordering labs, codes them to the patient's symptoms, not to URI.
CSS
  • Recognizes the cold visit and rooms the patient.
  • Documents the chief complaint from the patient's symptoms.
  • Performs any tests the provider orders.
The coding rule (provider step). When a lab is ordered during a URI visit, the order should not carry a URI diagnosis. It is coded to the patient's symptoms instead, with codes that begin with the symptom (for example, fever, cough, or headache). Since providers place lab orders here, this is the ordering provider's step.

Section 5

Coding Lab Orders

When a lab is ordered for a cold, it is coded to the symptom, not the URI. Since providers place lab orders here, this is their step.

The rule

When lab tests are ordered during a URI visit, the order should not carry a diagnosis of URI. It is coded to the patient's symptoms instead, with codes that begin with the symptom the lab is working up. At our clinic, providers place lab orders, so this is the ordering provider's responsibility.

Fever
Cough
Headache

These are examples. Use whichever symptom matches the reason the test is being run.

Why it matters

Keeping the URI diagnosis off lab orders leaves it where it belongs, in the provider's assessment, and keeps the episode coding clean. That way the measure reflects real URI visits and how they were treated, not the diagnosis attached to a routine lab order.

Two clean-coding habits, two roles. When the provider orders a lab, they code it to the patient's symptoms, never to URI. CSS keep their own piece clean by documenting the chief complaint accurately from what the patient reports. Together that keeps the data honest, so the measure shows the care that actually happened.

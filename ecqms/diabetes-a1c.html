eCQM: Diabetes A1c Control

An MA-driven workflow: catch a stale A1c before the visit, and get the result into the chart.
Section 1

Why It Matters

Diabetes is manageable, but only if the numbers are watched. As an MA, you are the one who makes sure they are.

Why the A1c matters

The hemoglobin A1c shows a patient's average blood sugar over about three months. Keeping it in range lowers the risk of the serious complications diabetes can cause, including vision loss, heart disease, stroke, kidney damage, and amputation. For a patient with diabetes, a current A1c is the single most important number at the visit.

A low rate is still a lot of people. Colorado has one of the nation's lowest diabetes rates, yet roughly 400,000 adults live with it, and most are not meeting all of their control goals. The visit in front of you is where that can change.

Why this is an MA-driven measure

Unlike a screen the provider performs in the room, this one runs on your prep. You catch the patients whose A1c is overdue, make sure they get tested, and get the result into the chart. Without that catch, a patient can go an entire year with no current number, which is bad for them and counts against the measure.

Section 2

The Measure

CMS-122 counts diabetic patients whose A1c is out of control, or never measured. Lower is better.

Measure Description · eCQM CMS-122

Percentage of patients 18 to 75 years of age with diabetes whose most recent hemoglobin A1c during the measurement period was greater than 9%. A missing result, or no A1c test at all during the period, also counts as poor control.

It is an inverse measure

For most measures, higher is better. This one is the opposite: a lower rate is better, because the measure counts patients in poor control. The goal is to keep patients out of that count by making sure each one has a recent A1c result that is in control.

A missing test counts against you. If a patient has no A1c result during the period, they are counted as poor control, exactly the same as an A1c over 9%. That is why getting a current result documented is the heart of this measure, and why your prep work moves the needle.

Two numbers, don't mix them up

Greater than 9% is the measure's poor-control threshold.
Greater than 7% is our clinical action goal for flagging.

Most patients with diabetes should aim for an A1c under 7%. We flag anyone above 7% (and overdue) so they get retested before they ever drift toward 9%. Acting at 7% keeps patients well clear of the measure's poor-control line.

Who is counted

  • Patients 18 to 75 years of age.
  • With a diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes.
  • Who had an eligible visit during the measurement period.

Section 3

Before the Visit

A quick check during prep catches the patients who are due, before they are ever roomed.

The A1c-Due Check

As you prepare for your patients, take a few seconds to look at the A1c. Here is the routine:

  1. Open the Labs component in the Ambulatory Organizer.
  2. Find the most recent A1c result and its date.
  3. Apply the rule below.
Flag the patient when BOTH are true. The last A1c result is greater than 7%, and the last result is more than 3 months old. If either one is not true, no flag is needed.

When both are true

Add "A1c-Due Check" to the comments on the Ambulatory Organizer. That comment is the reminder that tells the team to make sure this patient is tested during the visit. A few seconds in prep saves the result from being missed.

A strong answer would include

Both conditions apply: 7.8% is greater than 7%, and 8 months is more than 3 months old. So you add "A1c-Due Check" to the comments on Rosa's Ambulatory Organizer, so the team knows to make sure she is tested at today's visit.

Section 4

During the Visit

Confirm what is current, and make sure a result lands in the chart today.

Ask about outside testing

During the visit, ask the patient whether they have had an A1c done somewhere else, for example at another clinic, a specialist's office, or an outside lab. If they have, that result can count, and you will document it (next section).

Where the result can come from

  • Lab draw, either ours or an outside lab.
  • POC (point-of-care), a finger-stick A1c run in the clinic.

Both are valid sources of a result. What matters is that a current value reaches the chart.

Make sure it happens

If the patient was flagged with an A1c-Due Check and does not already have a recent result from elsewhere, make sure the A1c is collected today, by POC or by lab. If they do have a recent outside result, capture it and document it.

A current result is the goal. Whether it comes from your POC test, our lab, or an outside report the patient brings, the visit should not close without a current A1c in the chart for a patient who is due.

Section 5

Documenting It

Four ways a result reaches the chart. Each one updates the Recommendations Component.

How you document depends on where the result came from. In every case, the result needs to land on the Recommendations Component.

POC testing
Completing the HgbA1c POC form automatically updates the Recommendations Component.
Outside lab report (patient submitted)
Document the result value on the HgbA1c POC form (link available in the Recommendations Component). Enter the outside lab information in the comment field. Change the Performed on date to coincide with the report.
Patient stated
Still document on the HgbA1c POC form: mark it Done, enter the patient-stated date (close enough is fine, the exact time does not matter), and note where the patient had the test in the comment field.
RHC lab testing
Completed tests automatically update the Recommendations Component.
One chain, start to finish. The prep flag, the test, and the documentation are a single chain. The result only counts, for the patient and for the measure, once it is in the chart on the Recommendations Component. You are the one who closes the loop.
Apply ItRosa Linden, 64
Rosa hands you a printout from an outside lab: A1c 6.9%, dated about a month ago.

You have a real result and a real date. Now get it in cleanly.

Your turn: Walk through how you document Rosa's outside result so it lands correctly.

A strong answer would include

Open the HgbA1c POC form (the link is in the Recommendations Component), enter the result value of 6.9%, put the outside lab information in the comment field, and change the Performed on date to match the report (about a month ago). That records a current, in-control result on the Recommendations Component.

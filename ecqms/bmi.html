eCQM: Weight Assessment

Chart height and weight on every patient, every visit. It powers the BMI percentile measure for kids 3 to 17, and safe medication dosing at any age.
Section 1

Why It Matters

Growth is a vital sign for kids. Height and weight at every visit are how we track it.

Growth tells a story over time

For children, height and weight are not one-time numbers, they are points on a growth curve. Charted over time and read as a BMI-for-age percentile, they show whether a child is growing as expected, and they help the care team notice concerns early, in either direction, whether a child is not gaining enough or gaining too quickly.

A percentile is a growth signal, not a verdict. What matters most is the trend over time, in either direction. Charting height and weight at every visit is how that trend gets built, and it is the whole of the CMS-155a measure.

This is a staff-driven measure

Unlike measures that hinge on a provider's decision, this one runs on you. Rooming the patient and charting height and weight is what documents the BMI percentile and meets the measure. Simple, and almost entirely in your hands.

Section 2

The Measure

CMS-155a is met when a child's height, weight, and BMI percentile are documented.

Measure Description · eCQM CMS-155a

Percentage of patients 3 to 17 years of age with an eligible outpatient visit who had height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) percentile documented during the measurement period.

For kids, it is the percentile that counts

Because a healthy BMI range changes with a child's age and sex, this measure looks for the BMI percentile, not a raw BMI number. The good news: you do not calculate it. When you chart the height and weight, the system uses the child's age and sex to calculate the BMI and the BMI percentile automatically.

Chart the height and the weight. The BMI percentile follows.
One documented set in the year meets it. The measure looks for height, weight, and BMI percentile documented at least once during the measurement period. Charting at every visit is simply how you guarantee it is there.

Who is counted

  • Patients 3 to 17 years of age.
  • With an eligible outpatient visit during the measurement period.
  • Patients with a pregnancy diagnosis during the period are excluded.
The measure counts 3 to 17, but weigh everyone. Those ages are who the measure scores. Our practice is broader: chart height and weight on every patient, at every visit, because a current weight can be needed for medication dosing at any age. More on that in At Every Visit.

Section 3

At Every Visit

Chart height and weight as part of vitals, every time.

The routine

As you room the patient and take vitals, make height and weight part of the routine:

  1. Measure and chart the patient's height.
  2. Measure and chart the patient's weight.
  3. The system calculates the BMI and BMI percentile from the height, weight, age, and sex.

A current weight is a dosing tool

There is a reason to weigh at every visit that matters even more than the measure: medication dosing. Many medications, and for children most of them, are dosed by weight. The dose is calculated from the number on the scale today, not from a weight taken months ago. Kids grow quickly, so an old weight can be well off, and dosing from it can mean too little medicine to work or too much to be safe. Because weight can be needed for dosing at any age, chart height and weight on every patient, not only the 3 to 17 year olds the measure counts.

A fresh weight keeps dosing safe. When you chart a current weight, you are handing the provider and the pharmacy the accurate number they dose from. A stale weight can lead to an under-dose or an over-dose. For weight-based medications, the weight you capture today is a patient-safety check, not just a quality measure.
Every visit is an opportunity. You do not have to remember whether it was already done this year. If you chart height and weight at every visit, the BMI percentile is always current and the measure is always met.

Section 4

If the Patient Declines

If they would rather not be measured, a verbal report still counts. Chart it, and note the source.

Respect the patient, still capture the data

Some patients do not want to get on the scale or be measured for height. That is okay, and it is not worth a struggle. Instead, obtain a verbal report from the patient (or caregiver) of their height and weight.

Chart it, then note the source

Chart the height and weight as the patient provides them, and add a comment: "per patient's report." That records the data the measure needs while keeping the chart honest about where the numbers came from.

The comment matters. A verbal report charted with no note can look like a measured value. The comment "per patient's report" keeps the chart accurate about how the numbers were obtained, which protects both the patient's record and your documentation.

Section 5

Making It Count

Chart every visit, note the source when it is a report, and the measure takes care of itself.

The whole job, in three habits

  1. Chart height and weight at every visit, on every patient.
  2. Let the system calculate the BMI percentile from those values.
  3. If the patient declines to be measured, chart their verbal report and add "per patient's report."

If once is enough, why every visit?

The measure only needs one documented set in the year. But you rarely know in the moment whether it has already been captured, or whether an earlier value is stale. Charting at every visit removes the guesswork, keeps the growth curve complete, and means no eligible child is ever missed. And because weight-based medications are dosed from the most recent weight, weighing every visit protects the patient in a way that goes well beyond the measure.

Simple to meet, easy to miss. This is one of the most straightforward measures on the list, and one of the easiest to lose if height and weight get skipped. A few seconds at every visit, plus a quick comment when it is a verbal report, is the whole job.

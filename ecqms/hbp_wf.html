eCQM: High Blood Pressure

Accurate readings, in-office and home averaging, and the F/U-BP code. What CSS do to help patients and the measure.
Section 1

Why It Matters

High blood pressure is common and quiet. Controlling it prevents heart attacks and strokes.

Why control matters

High blood pressure usually has no symptoms, yet over time it damages the heart, brain, and kidneys. Keeping it controlled, under 140/90, is one of the most powerful ways to prevent heart attacks, strokes, and kidney disease. An accurate reading is where all of that starts, and that reading is yours to take.

The measure reads the most recent BP. CMS-165 counts a patient as controlled when their most recent reading in the period is under 140/90. So the reading you take, and a good second reading when the first is high, shapes both the patient's care and the measure.

Where CSS come in

As clinical support staff (CSS), you take the blood pressure, take a second reading and chart the lower of each when the first is high, and make sure BP follow-up visits are coded so they count. Accurate, consistent readings are the foundation of the whole measure.

Section 2

The Measure

CMS-165 counts hypertensive patients whose most recent BP is under 140/90.

Measure Description · eCQM CMS-165

Percentage of patients 18 to 85 years of age with a diagnosis of essential hypertension whose most recent blood pressure was adequately controlled (less than 140 AND less than 90 mmHg) during the measurement period.

Controlled means both numbers

A patient counts as controlled only when both numbers are in range: systolic under 140 and diastolic under 90. Higher is better for this measure. More controlled patients means a better score, and better-protected patients.

Under 140 and under 90 is controlled. 140/90 or higher is when we act.

The threshold is 140/90, not the red warning

This one is important. Cerner shows readings of 121/81 and above in red with a warning symbol. That red warning does not mean high blood pressure by our clinical guidelines, and it is not a trigger to take a second reading.

Act on the threshold, not the color. Take a second reading only for readings of 140/90 or higher (over 139 systolic or over 89 diastolic). The red warning at 121/81 and up is not our threshold, and treating it as one causes unnecessary repeats and patient anxiety.

Who is counted

  • Patients 18 to 85 years of age.
  • With a diagnosis of essential hypertension.
  • Counted as controlled when their most recent BP is under 140/90.

Section 3

In-Office BP Averaging

Take the first reading. If it is high, take a second and chart the lower of each.

As of 6/1/26, in-office blood pressure uses an averaging method. Here is the workflow:

  1. Take the first BP reading at the beginning of the visit.
  2. If the reading is under 140/90, chart it. No second reading is needed.
  3. If the reading is 140/90 or higher, write it down but do not chart it. Take a second reading.
  4. From the two readings, chart the lower systolic and the lower diastolic.
The lower of each. When there are two readings, chart the lower systolic and the lower diastolic. They can even come from different readings. A high first reading is never charted on its own.

Worked example

Reading 1 · do not chart
150/92
Reading 2
142/88
✓ Charted BP
142/88 mmHg

Lower systolic: the lower of 150 and 142 is 142. Lower diastolic: the lower of 92 and 88 is 88. So you chart 142/88.

A strong answer would include

Section 4

Home BP Averaging

An RN function. Home readings, averaged over several days, documented on the AdHoc form.

This is an RN function. The home BP averaging and its documentation are done by an RN. All staff should understand the workflow so you can support patients and recognize the documentation, but you are not expected to perform the averaging yourself.

Why home averaging

A home average reflects the patient's current control better than a single office reading. It reduces the variability of one-off readings, aligns with national guidance, and supports safer medication decisions.

How the average is calculated

  1. Select the data: the most recent 2 to 7 days of readings (follow provider instructions), at least one reading per day.
  2. Exclude Day 1: remove all readings from the first day of the window.
  3. Average: average all systolic values, then all diastolic values.
  4. Document on the Blood Pressure AdHoc form in Cerner: the average BP, the site, the date of the last reading, and a comment.
Home BP average: ___ / ___ mmHg based on X days of readings (Day 1 excluded). Date range: MM/DD/YY to MM/DD/YY.

Charting reminders: use the last date of the patient's BP entries as the date on the form, and enter the time as 0000. The minimum acceptable data is three days of readings.

Worked example

A patient brings 7 days of readings (April 1 to 7). Exclude April 1, then average April 2 to 7 (6 days):

Home BP average: 132/84 mmHg based on 6 days of readings (Day 1 excluded). Date range: 04/02/26 to 04/07/26.

Start a new monitoring period when

  • Medication is started or adjusted.
  • BP control is being reassessed.
  • A patient is newly instructed to monitor.

Common pitfalls to avoid

Using a single reading
Averaging only one day
Including Day 1
Using outdated readings
Not documenting the date range and method
Interpretation, and a best practice. A home BP average of 140/90 or higher is consistent with hypertension, the same clinic threshold. New home-monitoring patients should bring their device to the next visit so the team can confirm their technique. A Copilot tool to automate the averaging and documentation is coming soon.

Section 5

The F/U-BP Code

Code the Reason for Visit as F/U-BP so BP follow-up visits count. This is a supplement to the BP workflow.

The code to use

When scheduling a patient whose visit includes a blood pressure assessment or a hypertension follow-up, the Reason for Visit must be coded as F/U-BP. This is how the measure captures that a BP follow-up occurred. Without it, the visit may not count.

F/U-BP

Quick reference

Use
  • Reason for Visit: F/U-BP.
  • Enter it at scheduling, before the visit.
  • Confirm it at rooming if the visit includes a BP check.
Avoid
  • Generic codes: F/U, Office Visit, Follow up.
  • Leaving the Reason for Visit blank, or using a non-BP reason.
  • Assuming it will be corrected later.
Taking the BP is half the job. Coding the visit as F/U-BP is what lets the measure see it. Enter it at scheduling, and confirm it at rooming when the visit includes a BP check.
